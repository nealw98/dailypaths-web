# Daily Paths Theme Editorial Plan

Status: working editorial plan; proposed taxonomy and workflow, not a record of
what is currently deployed.

## 1. Purpose

Daily Paths daily readings meet a reader in one moment. Theme pages gather
related insights into a short, coherent journey that helps the reader:

1. Recognize their emotional experience.
2. Understand the recovery idea differently.
3. Identify what belongs to them and what does not.
4. Leave with one practical next step.

These pages are not encyclopedia entries, generic educational content, or long
SEO essays. They should feel like concise magazine features: emotionally
recognizable, spiritually direct, easy to scan, and grounded in Daily Paths
readings and Al-Anon principles.

The daily readings support the article. They should not determine or fragment
its structure.

## 2. Editorial voice

- Human, direct, and emotionally specific.
- First person when it makes the experience more recognizable.
- Spiritual language should be stated plainly. Do not qualify or apologize for
  references to a Higher Power.
- Avoid generic inspiration, clinical language, and AI-style summary prose.
- Do not present the author as an Al-Anon member when that is not true.
- Distinguish personal experience, Daily Paths interpretation, Al-Anon
  principles, and direct quotations from published literature.
- A page should sound like one coherent piece of writing, not a collection of
  individually polished ideas.

## 7. Magazine-style content system

Articles should be designed as reading experiences before prose is drafted.
Do not write a conventional long essay and add decorative components later.

### Recommended length

- Approximately 650–900 words of public article copy.
- Five to seven short prose sections.
- One or two memorable pull quotes.
- Zero or one useful list, comparison, table, or scenario group.
- One practical exercise or takeaway.
- Three to five connected Daily Paths readings.

These are guidelines, not quotas. A component must earn its place.

### Page storyboard

Before drafting, approve a one-page plan containing:

1. **Reader's emotional starting point** — What are they feeling or doing when
   they arrive?
2. **Opening hook** — A recognizable moment, thought, or conflict rather than a
   definition.
3. **Central insight** — What will the theme help them understand differently?
4. **Emotional journey** — What movement does the article make?
5. **Presentation choices** — Which ideas require prose, a pull quote, a list,
   a comparison, or a scenario?
6. **Practical ending** — What can the reader do today?
7. **Supporting readings** — Which Daily Paths readings deepen specific points?

### Article shapes

| Shape | Likely themes | Useful forms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emotional journey | Letting Go; Anger and Resentment; Fear and Worry; Guilt, Blame, and Shame; Getting Yourself Back | Recognizable opening, short narrative sections, pull quotes, reflection questions |
| Important distinction | Helping or Enabling?; Detachment with Love; Boundaries and Saying No | Comparisons, paired examples, decision questions, what-it-is/what-it-is-not distinctions |
| Daily practice | One Day at a Time; Gratitude; Higher Power and Trust | Short practices, numbered steps, reminders, brief situations |
| Orientation and reassurance | Living With Active Drinking; Alcoholism as a Disease; You're Not Alone | Clear explanations, common experiences, what helps, next-step links |

### Pull quotes

Use a pull quote only for a line worth remembering. Pull quotes should become a
recognizable Daily Paths signature rather than decoration.

Examples of suitable forms:

> Caring and carrying are not the same thing.

> It is not my job to break through their denial. It is my job to confront my
> own.

Prefer original Daily Paths language. Any quotation from published literature
must be brief, accurately attributed, and cleared for appropriate use.

### Insightful lists

Use a list when the ideas are genuinely parallel, such as:

- Signs I am trying to control an outcome.
- What belongs to me today.
- Ways fear disguises itself.
- What a boundary can protect.

Do not convert ordinary prose into bullets merely to make a page look shorter.

### Comparisons and tables

Use a comparison only when a reader needs to distinguish two easily confused
ideas. Strong candidates include:

- Helping versus enabling.
- Boundary versus punishment.
- Detachment versus indifference.
- Responsibility versus control.
- Guilt versus responsibility.

Do not invent a clever comparison and then force the content to fit it.

### Scenario cards

Use two or three brief situations when behavior is easier to recognize in
context. A scenario may show:

```text
What I used to do
-> What I recognize now
-> What I can do instead
```

Possible situations include a late-night call, an unpaid bill, monitoring a
phone or location, or another promise that things will change.

### Practical ending

Every article should end with something the reader can use:

- Three reflection questions.
- A short inventory.
- A five-minute practice.
- One boundary to consider.
- A meeting, sponsor, or fellowship next step.

The reader should leave with direction, not only understanding.

## 8. Editorial workflow

Treat the project as an editorial migration, not a blank-page project involving
fourteen articles and 366 manual classifications.

### Stage 1: Freeze the taxonomy

Draft and approve definitions in groups of three or four. The project owner
makes the final judgment; AI prepares a first pass.

### Stage 2: Maintain one editorial tracker

Suggested columns:

| Theme | Existing sources | Emotional hook | Central journey | Article shape | Components | Definition status | Article status | Reading status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Letting Go | Letting Go; Powerlessness | Preventing disaster has become second nature | Fear and control to dignity, trust, and one available action | Emotional journey | One pull quote; mine/not-mine distinction; short practice | Draft | Draft | Not started |

The tracker is the source of truth. Important editorial decisions should not
exist only in chat history.

### Stage 3: Complete direct conversions first

Start with existing material rather than the five blank-page articles:

1. Detachment with Love
2. One Day at a Time
3. Boundaries and Saying No
4. Higher Power and Trust
5. Gratitude
6. Alcoholism as a Disease
7. You're Not Alone
8. Letting Go

For each article:

1. Approve its internal theme definition.
2. Approve the page storyboard.
3. Draft and revise the prose in a fresh writing conversation.
4. Bring only approved copy into Codex.
5. Format and preview locally.
6. Publish only after explicit approval.

### Stage 4: Write the missing articles

Once the voice and component system are established, create the five new
focused articles and the merged Getting Yourself Back article.

### Stage 5: Classify readings

Do not manually classify 366 readings from a blank sheet. After definitions are
stable:

1. Read the full text of each reading.
2. Generate one primary theme and up to two supporting themes.
3. Record confidence and a short classification reason.
4. Flag ambiguous classifications.
5. Review low-confidence assignments, all three-theme assignments, confusable
   categories, distribution anomalies, and a random quality sample.
6. Export the approved mapping to CSV and dry-run the importer before changing
   Supabase.

Suggested review artifact:

```csv
date,slug,primary_theme,theme_2,theme_3,confidence,reason,review_status,notes
```

App feedback can help select or improve readings after classification. Ratings
and favorites must not determine theme membership.

## 9. Division of work

### Project owner

- Approves the taxonomy and editorial distinctions.
- Supplies lived observations and preferred language.
- Approves storyboards and final article copy.
- Reviews ambiguous reading assignments.

### Writing assistant

- Develops outlines and prose from a clean editorial brief.
- Preserves the approved voice and central journey.
- Does not edit site code or publish.

### Codex

- Inventories and maps existing content.
- Produces classification review artifacts.
- Implements approved copy and components.
- Runs local previews, link checks, and responsive QA.
- Commits and publishes only after explicit instruction.

### Anonymous Al-Anon reviewers

- Review program accuracy, tone, and potentially misleading claims.
- Do not need to surrender anonymity to provide editorial review.
- Their comments should be treated as review input, not attributed personal
  testimony unless separately submitted and approved for publication.

## 10. URL and migration safeguards

- Do not delete indexed existing pages merely because the taxonomy changes.
- Preserve useful URLs or provide deliberate permanent redirects.
- Supporting articles may remain available even when they are no longer
  top-level themes.
- Do not create duplicate active theme rows for renamed topics.
- Move existing essay content when changing a slug; inserting a new empty theme
  row is not sufficient.

The current local taxonomy migration needs review before it is applied. It
inserts `detachment-with-love` and `higher-power-and-trust` while the existing
`detachment` and `higher-power` rows may remain active. Without an explicit
content migration and redirect plan, that can produce duplicate topics and new
rows without the existing essay copy.
