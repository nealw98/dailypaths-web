Step Five: Being Honest About Our Wrongs

Building on the foundation laid in Step Four, where we took a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves, Step Five invites us to take an active leap toward healing. Step Five states that we "Admitted to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs".

For many of us in Al-Anon, the thought of exposing our past behaviors, hidden motives, and private thoughts can feel terrifying at first. Living with the family disease of alcoholism often taught us to hide our feelings, keep secrets, and wear a mask of perfection to protect ourselves. We may fear that if anyone knew the truth about us, they would reject or condemn us. Yet, Step Five shows us that keeping our secrets locked inside maintains our isolation and keeps us trapped in self-defeating patterns. By taking the risk to share our inventory honestly, we begin to break down the walls of shame and step into true personal freedom.

The Threefold Order of Admission

The specific structure of Step Five guides us through a gentle, intentional sequence of three admissions: to God, to ourselves, and to another human being.

Admitting to God: Placing God first allows us to "come clean" before the God of our understanding. Since our Higher Power already knows our hearts, this admission is not about informing God, but about opening ourselves up to a deeper, more intimate spiritual relationship without pretense or justification.

Admitting to Ourselves: Next, we face the truth of our inventory without whitewashing our errors or blaming others. Admitting to ourselves means taking full responsibility for our choices while also refusing to invalidate our talents and positive character assets. We simply acknowledge, "This is who I am".

Admitting to Another Human Being: Finally, we break our spiritual isolation by sharing our inventory aloud with a trusted person. Sharing our burden with another human being transforms abstract thoughts into concrete reality, freeing us from the heavy weight of hidden secrets.

Uncovering the "Exact Nature" of Our Wrongs

Step Five does not merely ask us to read a list of past mistakes or embarrassing moments; it asks us to examine the "exact nature" of our wrongs.

In Al-Anon, we learn to look beneath surface behaviors—such as outbursts of anger, hyper-vigilance, dishonesty, or people-pleasing—to identify the underlying motives, assumptions, and fears driving them. Upon closer examination, we often realize that many of our past errors were rooted in unspoken, self-defeating assumptions: the fear that we wouldn't have enough, the belief that we are not good enough, or the illusion that we must control everything to be safe. Recognizing these underlying patterns helps us understand that many of our defenses were misguided attempts to cope with unhealthy situations, allowing us to replace self-condemnation with self-compassion.

Choosing the Right Confidant and Finding Universal Connection

Because Step Five requires vulnerability, choosing the right person to hear our inventory is crucial. Al-Anon experience strongly suggests that it is usually unwise to share our Fifth Step with the alcoholic or a close family member, as they may be too emotionally involved or wounded by the events. Instead, most members select an Al-Anon sponsor, a trusted Al-Anon friend, a member of the clergy, or a professional counselor who understands the program and can listen with unconditional love and objectivity.

When we share our Fifth Step with a compassionate listener, a remarkable miracle often occurs: instead of experiencing the judgment or rejection we feared, we are met with understanding, warmth, and acceptance. Many of us are astonished to discover that our sponsor or confidant has experienced similar thoughts and feelings. In revealing what we thought made us uniquely flawed or unacceptable, we discover how human and alike we truly are.

Healing, Relief, and Reclaiming Our Worth

Step Five is not a tool for self-punishment or humiliation; it is a profound vehicle for spiritual healing and self-acceptance. By acknowledging both our shortcomings and our character assets in the presence of God and another person, we release the suffocating guilt of the past and make room for positive growth.

Completing Step Five brings a profound sense of relief, restored self-respect, and a renewed capacity for trust. We realize that our worth as human beings is not defined by our past mistakes, but by our willingness to heal and grow. Unburdened by secrets, we step out of darkness and into the light of fellowship, ready to continue our journey toward lasting serenity.
