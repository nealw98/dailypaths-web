Finding Help: Could Al-Anon Be for You?

Finding help when someone else’s drinking is affecting your life.

Is someone else’s drinking affecting your life? The answer may be obvious, or you may wonder whether things are serious enough to ask for help.

You might be keeping everything going while quietly worrying about tonight. Or the drinking stopped years ago, yet you still spend more time managing other people’s feelings than saying what you need. Start with what this is costing you.

How do I know when I need help?

How is someone else’s drinking affecting me?

The signs may be woven into ordinary days. You cancel dinner because you cannot count on someone arriving sober. You explain away another missed commitment. You spend an evening listening for the door instead of enjoying what you had planned.

Consider whether any of these questions sound familiar:

  • How often do I check on their drinking, whereabouts, or mood before deciding what I can do?
  • Have I taken on bills, responsibilities, or explanations because of what happens when they drink?
  • Am I rehearsing another conversation, hoping the right words will finally get through?
  • What have I stopped doing because I am worried about what might happen?
  • Who knows how much this is affecting me?

Worry, anger, and embarrassment can take up much of your day even while you keep up with work and family responsibilities. Notice how much of your life now revolves around someone else.

Al-Anon’s self-assessment for adults explores these experiences in more detail. Its guidance is that even one yes answer may be a reason to consider Al-Anon. Our questions here are prompts for reflection, not the official questionnaire or a diagnostic test.

They’re the one drinking. Why would I need help?

You may have put enormous effort into helping them. Asking for support yourself can feel beside the point: surely things would improve if they would just stop.

But you are living with the worry, exhaustion, and uncertainty now. You need somewhere to tell the truth about how you are doing. Talking it through can help you make a difficult choice or stop taking responsibility for things you cannot manage.

Asking for help does not mean you caused the drinking or failed the person you love. Nor does it commit you to a decision about the future of your relationship.

You need somewhere to tell the truth about how you are doing.

What if I grew up with the drinking?

Even if you left that home years ago and rarely talk about it, some of these questions may feel familiar:

  • When someone sounds disappointed, do I rush to make things right before considering what I think?
  • Do I keep agreeing to things because saying no feels worse than becoming overwhelmed?
  • When life is quiet, do I find myself waiting for something to go wrong?
  • Can I describe what everyone else needs more easily than what I want?

These questions can help you consider how growing up around drinking may still affect you; they do not establish the cause of a particular difficulty.

Al-Anon welcomes adults affected by a parent’s drinking. Its assessment for adults who grew up with a problem drinker explores approval, responsibility, relationships, and other experiences you may recognize. Adult Children of Alcoholics and Dysfunctional Families, introduced later in this guide, is another fellowship you can explore.

What if I’m a teenager?

It can be difficult to explain what is happening at home, especially when you care about the person who drinks. You may worry that speaking honestly will get them into trouble or make you seem disloyal.

Are you finding reasons to stay away from home? Do you act as though nothing bothers you because explaining feels too difficult? Is there something you wish you could tell an adult you trust?

You deserve support with what you are experiencing. You are not responsible for getting an adult sober or keeping the whole family together.

Alateen is part of Al-Anon and gives young people affected by someone else’s drinking a place to share with other young people. Start with the official teen self-assessment, or talk with a trusted adult such as a school counselor about finding support. If you are unsafe, tell a trusted adult who can help you get protection.

Is my situation serious enough?

On the good days, it can be easy to dismiss your concerns. The person keeps a job, cares about you, or meets many responsibilities. Hearing a more dramatic account from someone else can leave you thinking you have no right to complain.

You can love someone, recognize their good qualities, and still acknowledge the cost of their drinking. Being able to cope does not mean you have to keep coping alone.

You do not need to settle an argument about whether they are an alcoholic before exploring support. Al-Anon’s newcomer information begins with the effect of another person’s drinking on you.

Can I still need help after the drinking stops?

Yes. Sobriety may bring relief alongside questions about trust, old resentments, and how life together will change. You may still be checking, anticipating trouble, or waiting for the relationship to feel different.

Separation or loss can leave you with difficulties you still need to talk through. Support remains available when the person no longer drinks or is no longer in your life.

What help is available, and how do I begin?

What is Al-Anon, and how could it help me?

Al-Anon is a fellowship for people affected by someone else’s drinking. Members include partners, parents, adult children, other relatives, and friends. They share their experiences and use the program’s principles to work on their own lives, whether or not the person who drinks seeks help.

If you arrive hoping to find a way to change someone else, the focus on your own life may surprise you. What do you need? What choices are available to you?

For example, you might hear someone describe how they stopped making excuses for a relative’s absence, or how they learned to leave an argument and return to it later. Their circumstances may differ from yours, but their experience can help you consider a response you had not seen before.

Meetings, the Twelve Steps, literature, and contact with other members give you ways to work on those questions. A sponsor is a member who shares their experience of using the program and helps you apply it to your own life. You can learn about these parts of Al-Anon as you attend.

Read Al-Anon’s official introduction and its explanation of sponsorship.

Why do meetings matter?

Reading can help you recognize an idea. A meeting adds contact with people who are trying to live it.

Hearing someone describe an evening that sounds like yours can make it easier to admit feelings you have kept to yourself. Listen to how they handled it, what went wrong, and what they are learning. As you get to know people, there may be someone you want to talk with after the meeting.

The relationships may be different, yet the worries can be familiar. A parent concerned about an adult child may recognize the same urge to check and intervene that a partner describes. You can learn from the shared experience without needing everyone’s story to match your own.

Al-Anon describes this exchange of experience in its introduction for newcomers.

You can learn from the shared experience without needing everyone’s story to match your own.

What happens at an Al-Anon meeting?

Formats differ. A meeting may include readings, a topic, or a member speaking about their experience, followed by time for others to share. You can listen without speaking, and you do not need a prepared account of your family.

There are no membership dues or fees. Groups may invite voluntary contributions toward expenses. Anonymity and confidentiality are program principles; members are expected to protect whom they see and what they hear. Ask the group about online privacy practices if you are unsure.

You can ask questions before or after the meeting. Al-Anon’s newcomer FAQ answers common concerns about attendance, cost, privacy, and participation.

Do I have to be religious?

Al-Anon is spiritual rather than religious. People of any faith or none are welcome. You will encounter references to God and a Higher Power, with room for each person’s own understanding.

You do not have to resolve your beliefs before attending. The official FAQ explains this approach.

How do I find a meeting?

Start with a meeting you can realistically attend. Getting out of the house may matter to you; avoiding a long drive or finding a workable time may matter more.

In-person meetings bring people together in a shared room. Online meetings let you join from your own location; many use video, while other formats are available. Hybrid meetings combine an in-person gathering with electronic access, so check how you can participate. Telephone meetings use a dial-in number rather than requiring a video connection.

  • In person or hybrid: Use the official Al-Anon meeting finder and read the attendance details.
  • Online or by telephone: Explore Al-Anon’s meeting options for the relevant directories.
  • Help finding the right contact: Use Worldwide Al-Anon Contacts to locate a local information service or office.
  • For a young person: Begin with Alateen’s official information and check the meeting’s age and access arrangements.

If the listing leaves you unsure, a simple question to the group contact is enough: “I’ve never attended before. What do I need to know about joining this meeting?”

How long should I give it?

A first meeting can feel unfamiliar. You may be listening through worry, comparing your circumstances with everyone else’s, or trying to understand new language.

Al-Anon’s website explains that most groups suggest attending at least six meetings to allow time to become familiar with the approach. Read its guidance about newcomers and meetings for the explanation.

Try different groups if the first does not feel like a good fit. A quieter discussion, a different time, or another format may suit you better. Look for what is becoming useful: a person you can talk to, an idea you remember during an argument, or a growing ability to describe how you are doing.

Can I read something before I go?

Yes. Reading can make the language more familiar and give you a question to bring to a meeting.

Al-Anon’s Conference Approved Literature page offers information and samples from its publications. Its First Steps podcasts introduce common newcomer questions through audio.

You do not need to finish a book before attending.

Are there other programs I could explore?

Two other fellowships may be useful, depending on what you want help with:

Adult Children of Alcoholics and Dysfunctional Families (ACA) focuses on recovering from the effects of growing up in an alcoholic or otherwise dysfunctional family. Its Welcome Path introduces the program and ways to begin.

Co-Dependents Anonymous (CoDA) focuses on developing healthy relationships. If recurring difficulties in relationships are what you want to explore, its newcomer information provides an introduction.

These are separate fellowships, not branches of Al-Anon. Their newcomer pages can help you decide which meetings to explore.

What if I need other kinds of help?

A fellowship offers shared experience and ongoing support. Some needs also call for professional or practical assistance: a counselor to work with you individually, a healthcare professional for health concerns, or a qualified adviser for housing, financial, or legal questions.

Professional help and Al-Anon can be part of the same plan.

When safety is at risk

Threats, violence, coercion, or concern for a child’s safety call for direct help. You do not have to wait for meetings or someone else’s recovery before seeking protection.

If you are in immediate danger in the United States, call 911. For support with relationship abuse and safety planning, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline: 800-799-SAFE (7233) or text START to 88788. If your device may be monitored, use a safer device if possible. Outside the U.S., contact your local emergency or domestic abuse service.

What is one step I can take today?

Choose one step today: read the assessment that fits your situation, contact a local group, or save the details of a meeting you can attend.

If you are ready to go, you can keep your introduction simple: “I’m new. Someone’s drinking is affecting me, and I’d like to listen.”

Between meetings, a Daily Reflection can give you an idea to consider, a question to discuss, or a choice to practice that day.
