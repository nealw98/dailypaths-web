The Stories We Tell Ourselves

Fear of rejection, uncertainty, and not being enough can shape the stories we tell ourselves. An exploration of seeing the facts and letting others help us face them.

By Neal W.

Someone cancels at the last minute, and we tell ourselves we didn’t want to go anyway. We cook a special dinner, and when they don’t show up, we excuse it by saying they’re busy at work. Someone’s drinking spoils another family gathering, and by morning we have an explanation for why this time was unusual.

We can be remarkably generous with explanations and excuses when we’re afraid of what people might think. Or what we might have to admit to ourselves.

Perhaps we’d have to admit how lonely we are, how hurt we felt, or how embarrassed we feel about what happened. Perhaps we’d have to face a disagreement we’ve been avoiding. Underneath it all may be a fear that is harder to say aloud: if we stop making allowances, ask for more, or let someone see how much we need them, they’ll reject us.

“It can feel normal not to know our own temperature, only theirs.”

That fear reaches well beyond anyone’s drinking. It can follow us into a friendship, a marriage, a meeting, or a room full of people we want to like us. The stories we tell often begin as a way to hide the truth from others. We want to look like we’ve got it together because we’re afraid to admit we’re struggling on the inside.

The explanations we need to believe

“They’ve been under a lot of pressure” may be true. But that pressure might be the consequences of their drinking. We say it to hide our disappointment. We dismiss the situation with a simple phrase that means we don’t have to explain our own experience.

Seeing things as they really are can be painful. We may have spent years believing things would settle down once work improved, the children were older, or the drinking stopped. There’s comfort in having a reason to wait. It postpones the question of what we would do if things stayed as they are.

“It lets us change without hating ourselves.”

Minimization, rationalization, and justification are the tools of denial. They help us avoid seeing the truth, telling the truth, or accepting the truth. Their language is familiar enough to pass unnoticed, especially when we’re the ones speaking.

How we explain it away

Minimization — making the impact seem smaller

“It was only one missed dinner.”

Rationalization — finding a reasonable-sounding explanation

“Work has been stressful. Things will settle down.”

Justification — defending a choice so we don’t have to examine it

“I had to cover for them. What else could I do?”

“I had no choice” can be particularly comforting. It spares us from making a choice we dislike: saying no, allowing someone to be disappointed, or admitting we couldn’t manage everything. We may have been afraid of their reaction. We may also have been afraid of who we would be if we were no longer the person who always understood, always helped, always made it work.

An explanation doesn’t erase the impact.

When fear fills in the blanks

The story doesn’t always comfort us. Sometimes fear supplies an explanation that hurts more than the uncertainty did.

A message gets a brief reply, and we read it several times, looking for what we did wrong. A friend spends time with someone else, and we wonder whether we’ve become a burden. Before anyone has rejected us, we’re already trying to make ourselves easier to keep around.

If we’re afraid we aren’t good enough, ordinary gaps in information can seem to confirm it. We apologize without knowing whether we’ve done anything wrong. We rehearse an argument about something the other person may never have thought. By the time we speak to them, we’ve been living inside that conversation for hours.

My first sponsor taught me to begin every difficult decision with a question: “What are the facts?” It’s a useful question when we’re certain we know what someone else is thinking, planning, or feeling.

A text goes unanswered. We feel hurt. But the fact is we don’t know why it hasn’t been answered. Keeping those things separate can be surprisingly difficult when the explanation feels so convincing. Our fear is real; the motive we’ve assigned to someone else is still a guess.

Sometimes we need more facts. There’s honesty in “I don’t know.” And one of the biggest things we don’t know is the future. We imagine that we understand all the possible outcomes of a decision. The truth, however, is that our Higher Power can create an outcome we couldn’t imagine.

Saying it where someone can hear

Something I heard in a meeting about thirty years ago has stayed with me:

“We tell others the truth because it makes it harder to lie to ourselves.”

There’s a difference between privately knowing we spent the weekend waiting and saying it to someone who cares about us. We hear ourselves describe the canceled plans, the checking, the excuses. We may start to add “but it wasn’t that bad” and realize how much work that little phrase is doing.

A sponsor remembers what we said last week, before we found a more comfortable way to explain it. Someone in a meeting describes making the same excuses, and we recognize our own reasoning before we have time to defend it. The recognition can be embarrassing. It can also be a relief to hear from someone who has thought the same thing or done the same thing.

We often find help in that shared experience. The person who has been through something similar may see a pattern more clearly because they’re no longer in the middle of it. They help us see through our story, just as they had to see through theirs.

What we already know

More information won’t always resolve the part that hurts. We may never know exactly why someone keeps breaking a promise. We may know quite clearly that we no longer trust it.

You don’t have to know what someone is thinking to acknowledge how their behavior affects you.

That acknowledgment can expose another story: if we understand them well enough, choose the right words, or become patient enough, we can make the relationship turn out the way we need it to. As long as the answer lies in something we haven’t tried yet, we can keep believing we control the outcome.

Accepting our powerlessness means facing the possibility that our best effort won’t change another person. There can be grief in that. We may have loved them, helped them, and done everything we knew how to do. We may still need boundaries we hoped would never be necessary.

When you catch yourself telling a story

  • Stop mind-reading. We can see what someone does and hear what they say. We can’t know what they’re thinking or feeling without asking—and sometimes we still won’t know.
  • Don’t argue with someone who isn’t there. An imagined conversation lets us supply both sides. We can work ourselves into anger or hurt over words the other person hasn’t said.
  • Put away the crystal ball. We tell stories about the future to explain or justify what we’re doing now. We can prepare for what might happen without telling ourselves we know what will.
  • Look at the facts. What do we know is true, and what do we only think is true? If we need more information, we can ask instead of filling in the blanks.
  • Say the story out loud. Tell a sponsor or someone we trust what happened and what we’ve made it mean. Someone who’s been there may recognize the fear or excuse we can’t yet see.
  • Face the fear and tell the truth. “I felt hurt.” “I’m afraid you’ll reject me.” “I’ve been pretending this doesn’t bother me.” With someone we trust, that honesty may bring the love and understanding we were afraid to ask for.
  • Let the truth change what we do. Once we recognize what’s happening, we can stop making the excuse, have the real conversation, or follow through on a boundary. We don’t have to control the other person’s response to be honest about our own lives.

    • We’ll never be perfect. We’ll still make assumptions and find excuses. We can admit we’ve been wrong without making it proof that we’re not enough. Learning to recognize the stories we tell takes time, and we’ll sometimes need someone else to help us see them. Each time we become willing to look again and tell the truth, we’re practicing recovery.
