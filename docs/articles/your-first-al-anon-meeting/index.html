Your First Al-Anon Meeting: What to Expect

A welcoming, practical introduction to attending a first meeting: whether you belong, what happens, whether you need to speak, and what you might take away.

By Daily Paths

You may have found a meeting and still be unsure about going. Which door do you use? Will someone ask you to explain why you are there? What if you start crying and cannot finish a sentence?

You do not need to turn your life into a clear story before you attend. You can spend your first meeting listening. The customs and language will become more familiar as you hear people use them.

Arriving or joining online

For an in-person meeting, check the listing for the room and entrance as well as the address. Arriving a few minutes early gives you time to find your way. If people are already talking, a simple “Is this the Al-Anon meeting? It's my first time” can help you start a conversation without having to explain anything more.

Al-Anon does not require a referral or appointment. You can use your first name; there is no need to give the room your full identity or family history. The official newcomer FAQ explains these basics.

If you are joining online, allow a few minutes to connect and check your sound. Read the group's instructions about joining, cameras, and participation. A private place to listen helps protect everyone's conversation. If you are unsure how to take part, you might ask the host, “I'm new—how do people let you know they want to speak?”

What happens during the meeting

A member generally leads the meeting. Many groups begin with a quiet moment and the Serenity Prayer, followed by a welcome and readings about Al-Anon. There may be introductions and a few announcements before the main discussion. Al-Anon's suggested meeting format offers a common outline, while leaving room for groups to do things differently.

A speaker may describe their experience, or members may discuss a reading, a Step, or a topic such as resentment or acceptance. “Sharing” means talking about their own experience and what they are learning.

Meetings generally give people room to speak without interruption or advice. If you describe something painful and the next person shares their own experience, that can feel strange. You may have expected questions, suggestions, or an answer to your problem.

The practice is to listen and share experience, leaving each person to decide what applies to their own life. Someone's account of a difficult conversation may give you an idea to consider without telling you what to do. Al-Anon's guidance for groups encourages careful listening and avoiding advice.

You do not need to turn your life into a clear story before you attend.

Listening—and speaking if you choose

Speaking is optional. If you are invited to share, “I'm here to listen today” is enough. You can hear how others participate before deciding what you want to say.

If you do speak, begin with what brought you there: “I've been spending so much time worrying about someone else's drinking that I don't know what I need anymore.” If the words are hard to find, “I'm nervous, and I'm not sure where to start” is a beginning too.

Talk about what is troubling you today rather than trying to cover the whole history. If you become tearful, take your time. Pause to breathe, or say you would rather stop and listen for now.

As you listen, you may hear circumstances very different from yours. Someone may be talking about a parent while you are worried about a partner or child. Notice whether you recognize a feeling or a habit: waiting for the phone to ring, making excuses, or being unable to concentrate on anything else. The details do not have to match for something to be useful.

At my first meeting

I was very emotional in my first meeting. I wanted so bad to hear how to fix the alcoholic in my life. What I heard were people who were talking about themselves and their own lives. I heard them talk about what they had done for themselves how they had made changes in their own life where they had detached themselves from the alcoholic in their lives. What I left the meeting that day was people who had been hurt people who were angry talk about their recovery from the disease of alcoholism that had controled their life. I realized that they were talking about me. It wasn’t what I expected but the most important thing that I took away from my first meeting was hope. I came away feeling a little bit better than when I walked in. It was the feeling of hope, that there was hope for me kept me going back. They said to me at the end of the meeting to “ keep coming back it gets better.”

I did and it does!

— Lance W.

Words and customs that may feel unfamiliar

Hearing “God” or “Higher Power” may raise questions, especially if religion has been difficult for you or has never been part of your life. Al-Anon describes itself as spiritual and welcomes people of any faith or none. Each member is free to understand a Higher Power in their own way. Listen to how members describe that help in their own lives.

The readings can also sound unfamiliar when others seem to know them by heart. Give yourself time to hear what they mean. You might come back to one sentence later, after something in the discussion helps it make sense.

A few words you may hear

The Steps: Twelve principles that members practice over time as they work on their own recovery.

A sponsor: Another Al-Anon member who shares their experience of the program with you personally.

Anonymity: Protecting members' identities. Confidentiality also means keeping other people's stories within the meeting.

“Keep coming back”: An invitation to give yourself more time with the program.

Al-Anon's welcome for newcomers explains more about its spiritual approach, sponsorship, and privacy.

When a basket is passed around, the contributions help pay group expenses. Giving is voluntary; there are no dues or fees to attend.

Privacy is a shared responsibility: do not repeat who attended or what someone shared. Online, check what name your account displays and who can hear the meeting around you. Platforms differ, so ask the group if you have a privacy question.

When the meeting ends

The group may finish with a closing reading, prayer, or shared statement. Listen as others take part.

Members may stay to talk afterward, and Al-Anon encourages newcomers to ask questions before or after meetings. If something someone said stayed with you, that can be an opening: “I recognized what you said about worrying all the time. Could I ask you about it?” Or you could ask about a reading or word you did not understand. Online, ask whether there is time for conversation after the formal meeting ends.

You might leave relieved, or go home unsure what to make of it. Hearing people talk openly about things you rarely discuss can be tiring. Give yourself some time before deciding whether the meeting was useful.

The details do not have to match for something to be useful.

Groups differ in their format and atmosphere. Al-Anon suggests trying at least six different meetings before deciding whether the program is for you. That gives you a chance to hear different people and become more familiar with the experience. You do not have to describe the first meeting as wonderful to consider going again.

For help finding another meeting or exploring other support, see Finding Help: Could Al-Anon Be for You?. A Daily Reflection can give you a question to bring to your next conversation.

Choose a meeting to try next, or ask someone you met whether you could talk again.
