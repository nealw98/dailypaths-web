Detachment with Love

Caring about someone whose drinking affects you without making their life your responsibility.

In this guide

When their life takes over yours

You listen for a change in their voice, watch their face when they come through the door, or lie awake rehearsing the conversation that might finally get through to them. If they seem happy, you relax. If they are angry, you wonder what you did wrong.

After a while, you make decisions around problems that haven't happened yet. You turn down an invitation because they might need you. You keep good news to yourself because they are having a bad day. Even when nothing is happening, you are waiting.

Detachment begins with recognizing how much of your life this has taken over. It means giving up the job of managing another person's drinking, moods, and choices so you can attend to your own. That applies whether the person is your partner, parent, adult child, sibling, or friend. They don't have to live with you to occupy most of your thoughts.

I don’t have to live on the edge of every decision you make.

What detachment with love means

Al-Anon's Detachment leaflet describes separating ourselves from the harmful effects of another person's alcoholism. Stepping back helps us see what is happening more clearly and turn our attention to our own well-being. It does not necessarily mean physical separation.

Distance alone doesn't settle the problem. You can hang up the phone and spend the next three hours arguing with the person in your head. You can refuse to speak while waiting for an apology. Their reaction is still deciding how you spend your evening.

With detachment, you begin to give that attention back to your own life. You can care about someone without supervising them. You can understand that alcoholism is an illness and still refuse harmful behavior. You can keep your plans even when they are unhappy.

“With love” includes respect for another person's right to make choices you would not make for them. In Al-Anon, this also means learning to trust a Higher Power with what you cannot control and seeking guidance for what you can do. Their recovery cannot be your assignment.

Practicing detachment

Understanding the idea is easier than following through when the phone rings. Start with a situation that keeps repeating.

Pause before responding

Notice what is pushing you to act. Are you afraid of what might happen, guilty about saying no, or determined to make them understand? A feeling can be strong without telling you what to do. Unless the situation is urgent, put down the phone or say you need time to consider the request.

The pause gives you a chance to decide whether anything is yours to do before agreeing to do it.

Stop the circular argument

You have explained your point several times. Now you are correcting each accusation and waiting for them to admit you are right. Ask yourself what another round will accomplish.

“We are going in circles. I'm stopping this conversation for now.” End the call or step away when it is safe. An issue that needs resolving can be addressed later; you don't have to keep arguing until the other person agrees to stop.

You don’t have to convince someone you’re right before you walk away from an argument.

Leave consequences where they belong

An adult sibling who misses work can contact their own employer. If you supply an excuse, you take on the task and hide the connection between their drinking and its consequences.

Stepping back means allowing consequences you have repeatedly prevented. It does not mean arranging a crisis or waiting for suffering to force someone into recovery. You cannot know whether a particular consequence will lead them to seek help. Immediate danger requires action; see the separate safety section below.

Resume your own life

Keep the appointment you would usually cancel. Eat a meal without checking your phone throughout it. See the friend you have been putting off. Concern may come along with you, but it doesn't have to cancel everything else.

Pay attention to sleep, food, and company as well. These needs are easy to neglect when each new problem seems more pressing than your own day.

Use the help available

At an Al-Anon meeting, you can hear how others handle situations like yours. A sponsor, an experienced member who helps you work the program, can help you examine a decision without making it for you.

Use the Serenity Prayer to ask what you need to accept and what is yours to change. When you cannot tell, ask your Higher Power for guidance, talk with someone, attend a meeting, or read program literature. You do not have to settle every difficult decision alone.

Caring without taking over

You can refuse a request and still be kind. You don't have to produce a warm feeling first; a brief, respectful answer may be all you can manage that day.

The blanket

In a familiar Al-Anon story, a woman shared that her husband often fell out of bed after drinking. She felt responsible for getting him back into bed, straining her back as she lifted him night after night.

After hearing about detachment at meetings, she proudly reported that the next time it happened, she had simply stepped over him and climbed into bed.

The response from the group helped her see another possibility: that was detachment, but where was the love?

The next time, she still didn't lift him back into bed. But before getting into bed herself, she covered him with a blanket.

She had found a way to stop rescuing him while still showing care. The blanket asked her to give neither her back nor her night's rest—just a little kindness.

You might decline another loan and still invite your sibling to lunch. You might sit beside your adult child while they make a difficult call, leaving them to speak. Help becomes taking over when you arrange the appointment, make the call, and check afterward to see whether they followed through.

There is also room for companionship that has nothing to do with solving a problem. Where contact is safe and welcome, enjoy the meal or the game. Every conversation need not become a discussion about drinking.

When guilt, fear, or pressure returns

Saying no may leave you feeling worse at first. Before changing your answer, examine the guilt. Did you neglect a responsibility or speak cruelly? Or is someone simply unhappy with your decision? Those call for different responses. You can apologize for how you spoke without agreeing to do the task.

Take fear seriously enough to examine it, too. Is someone in immediate danger, or are you trying to prevent an outcome you cannot control? Get help assessing the situation when you are unsure.

Other family members may want you to keep doing what they have come to expect. Explain what you will do, but don't turn the explanation into a request for everyone's approval.

You will sometimes check again, resume the argument, or agree to another rescue. Look at what drew you back in and talk it through with someone who understands. Grief may be part of this work: grief for the relationship you wanted or the years spent trying to change it. Feeling relief doesn't rule out sadness.

When they are sober

Sobriety can give you a new set of things to monitor. Are they attending enough meetings? Why are they irritable? Should you avoid raising a problem in case it upsets them?

Supervising recovery can become as consuming as monitoring drinking. Their recovery is still theirs to pursue. Your concerns still deserve to be heard, and trust will take more than a change in drinking to rebuild.

In Learning to Trust, Celina R. describes facing her own anger and fear after her husband's sobriety, and how Al-Anon helped her begin to change.

Detachment and safety

Safety comes first

Immediate danger or suspected overdose: call 911 in the United States.

If someone cannot be awakened or has slow or irregular breathing, get emergency help. Do not assume they can sleep it off. Learn the warning signs of alcohol overdose from NIAAA.

If opioid overdose is possible, give Narcan (naloxone) if available and call 911 immediately. Follow the product instructions and the dispatcher’s guidance. Stay until help arrives, even if they wake up. Narcan reverses opioid overdose; it does not treat alcohol poisoning. Learn about naloxone from the CDC.

Detachment does not mean accepting threats, violence, or unsafe care of children. You do not have to remain in a relationship or living arrangement to demonstrate love. If you fear someone’s reaction, get help making a safety plan.

National Domestic Violence Hotline: Call 800-799-SAFE (7233) or visit thehotline.org.

Begin with the next decision

Choose one responsibility you have been taking over and decide how you will handle it next time. Ask for help following through. Over time, doing less managing can leave you less resentful and more able to enjoy the person you care about. You can begin before they change.
