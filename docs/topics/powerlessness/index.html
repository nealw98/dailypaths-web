Surrendering the Unwinnable Battle

Stop trying to manage another person's drinking or recovery. Put that effort into the life you can change: your own.

The struggle to keep everything together

You have explained the problem so often that you know the conversation by heart. You plead, argue, and plan for what might happen next. Another promise gives you hope. Another broken promise sends you looking for something you haven't tried.

The work can continue after the drinking stops. Now you count meetings, watch for changes in mood, and hold back concerns because you don't want to threaten their recovery. You are still on duty.

Surrender begins with admitting that this job is beyond your power. You cannot make another person stop drinking or commit to recovery. Continuing to organize your life around that effort costs you time, health, and relationships without giving you control.

Accept that limit and let it change what you do next.

Understanding surrender

Step One of Al-Anon states: “We admitted we were powerless over alcohol—that our lives had become unmanageable.”

Admitting powerlessness can feel like abandoning someone you love. As long as you are trying, you have something to do with the fear. Stopping means facing how little control you have had all along.

Caring has never given you the power to supply another person's willingness.

You can speak honestly about what the drinking is doing to your household and offer help you are willing to give, but the decision to seek recovery remains theirs. Leave that decision with them, including when they make a choice you fear.

The admission that your way is not working opens a practical question: how will you live now? Al-Anon offers help with that question. You need a way to live that does not depend on getting someone else to change first.

Spiritual Principles of Surrender

Surrender requires a spiritual change in how we live: giving up the belief that our own thinking and willpower can control the alcoholic. Surrender is the first step to becoming willing to rely on a Power greater than ourselves. Honesty, acceptance, and humility are how we put that reliance into practice. These principles ask us to face reality, acknowledge our limits, and become willing to receive help.

  • Honesty: Looking truthfully at our situation and giving up the blanket of denial. Our efforts have not given us control over the drinking and never will.
  • Acceptance: Seeing the situation as it is today, not as we wish it were. Recognizing how our efforts to manage the alcoholic and the consequences of their drinking have made our own lives unmanageable. We feel responsible for preventing what we cannot control, guilty when we cannot prevent it, and frustrated when our efforts fail. Accepting that limit brings the focus back to our own recovery.
  • Humility: Admitting that we cannot resolve our unmanageability through our own thinking and willpower alone. We need help from a Power greater than ourselves—and the willingness to seek that help.

    • Put responsibility where it belongs

    The Three Cs address the blame that keeps many of us trying to fix the drinking.

    When someone says they drank because of something you did, it can be hard to examine your conduct without accepting the whole accusation. Be honest about what you regret and apologize for it. That apology does not make their drinking your responsibility, and their alcoholism is not evidence that you failed to love them well enough.

    Recognizing unmanageability

    You may still be going to work, paying the bills, and keeping the house in order. Look at what it takes to get through the day. Are you sleeping? Can you concentrate? Have you stopped seeing friends because you are too preoccupied to enjoy them?

    Unmanageability includes the life you keep postponing until the alcoholic is doing better. It also includes the resentment that spills into other situations: an explosion over dishes in the sink, impatience with a friend, or another evening when you cannot settle to anything.

    Take this seriously before the next crisis. The way you are living deserves attention even if the other person sees no problem with it.

    Practicing surrender

    Choose one recurring situation. Work through these five steps using that situation, rather than making a general promise to let go.

    1. Name the outcome you are trying to control

    “I want them to be well” is too broad. Name the result you are working to produce: “I want them to admit they drank,” or “I want to make sure they attend a meeting.” Then name what you are doing to get it: repeated questions, checking, reminders, or recruiting relatives to persuade them.

    Be specific enough to recognize the behavior when you start doing it again.

    2. Admit the limit of your power

    Look at the results of your efforts. Have they given you lasting control over the drinking? Have your reminders produced willingness, or only another round of reminders?

    “I want them to get help. I cannot make them want it.” Let that answer guide your decision. Another explanation of what they already know will not give you authority over their recovery.

    3. Decide what you will stop doing

    Choose one action that keeps you in the struggle and stop doing it. If you already know drinking is a problem, another search for evidence can consume your evening without changing what you know. Repeating advice they have heard or calling their employer to explain away a hangover gives you another job in managing their drinking. Decide which of these jobs you are giving up.

    An argument can keep you occupied in the same way. Once you have stated your position and the conversation is going in circles, end your part in it. You do not have to finish presenting your case before you stop.

    4. Take an action that belongs to you

    • Argue back

      Suppose your partner announces that they are skipping a recovery meeting. Before you know it, you are explaining why they need to go, finding other meeting times, and planning to check afterward. Leave their attendance with them and go to your own meeting as planned.

    • Defend myself

      You may worry throughout the drive. Even so, you are spending the evening getting help with your own recovery instead of supervising theirs. Apply that same choice to the appointment you keep canceling, the unfinished work, or the meal you have been putting off. These are parts of your life that need your attention now.

    • Revisit the past

      For help deciding on limits, use the Boundaries guide. The Detachment guide addresses caring without taking over.

    • Stay in the chaos

      5. Arrange support before you reverse the decision

    Tell your sponsor or a trusted person what you are stopping and where you expect trouble. “I'm going to want to call and check. Can I talk to you before I do that?” gives them something concrete to help with.

    • Pause

      Use that support when the urge returns. Don't wait until you have already spent the evening investigating or arguing.

      When you want to take control back

    • Protect my peace

      Fear is still there

    Surrender can leave you frightened. Doing something familiar may promise immediate relief, even when it has failed to change the situation before. Do not use that relief as your measure of a sound decision.

    A prayer or a call to someone who understands can help you stay with the decision long enough to get on with your day. Check HALT: are you Hungry, Angry, Lonely, or Tired? If you have missed lunch or spent the day alone with your worries, attend to that need. Exhaustion and isolation make it harder to follow through.

    You are waiting for surrender to change them

    You stop intervening and start watching. Have they noticed? Are they worried about losing your help? Have they finally decided to change? When nothing happens, you conclude that surrender didn't work.

    You are still pursuing the same result. This time, withholding help is the method. Give up the bargain that says you will attend to your life if they respond the way you want.

    Their recovery cannot be the condition you place on attending to your own life.

    Judge your practice by your conduct. Did you tell the truth, keep your plans, and leave their decisions with them? Keep doing those things even if the drinking continues.

    Consequences affect you too

    Surrender offers no promise that their next choice will be a good one. Seeing trouble ahead does not give you the power to prevent every consequence.

    Work out what actually requires your action. If a missed shift puts the household income at risk, you need a plan for the bills; you do not need to invent an excuse for their employer. Children still need reliable care, regardless of who caused the problem. Protect those necessities and get help with decisions you cannot sort through alone. Being responsible for your household does not make you responsible for keeping another adult sober.

    Surrender and Safety

    Call 911 if you are in immediate danger in the United States.

    Accepting powerlessness does not require you to remain in danger. Ending your part in an argument does not guarantee that someone else will stop threats or abuse.

    If you fear violence or retaliation, seek help making a safety plan. In the United States, contact the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 800-799-SAFE (7233) or text START to 88788.

    Guilt brings you back

    Before resuming a task, ask what you owe the person. A commitment you made needs to be honored or discussed, and a cruel remark calls for an apology. But if the guilt comes from watching them struggle with something you used to handle, taking over only restores the arrangement you decided to change. Their disappointment is not a new obligation.

    You catch yourself controlling again

    Stop when you notice it, even halfway through the lecture. You don't have to finish an argument to justify starting it. Take the specific incident to your sponsor or a meeting: what frightened you, what you did, and what you will try next time.

    Support for daily practice

    Make meetings and contact with other members part of your routine. You need people who can hear the whole story and help you recognize your part in it.

    This is where the timeworn slogans on the wall provide real help:

    Let Go and Let God. Turn the outcome over to a Higher Power and ask for guidance about your own conduct. If belief is uncertain, begin with willingness to seek help beyond your own thinking.

    One Day at a Time. Deal with today. You don't have to work out how you will handle every crisis you can imagine.

    Keep It Simple. Name the decision in front of you. Decide what is yours to do and do that.

    Easy Does It. Slow down before fear commits you to another task. Take time to think before you answer or act.

    Ask for the wisdom to know the difference

    The Serenity Prayer gives us a place to begin:

    “God grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, courage to change the things I can, and wisdom to know the difference.”

    That last request matters. Fear and love can make taking over feel like a duty. When you cannot tell what belongs to you, ask your Higher Power for guidance and take the question to a sponsor, trusted Al-Anon friends, a meeting, or program literature. Use the help you are asking for.

    Surrender means taking your hands off the alcoholic's life and putting your attention back on your own.

    Choose one controlling action to stop today, with a plan for what you will do instead and whom you will call when you want to go back. The outcome will still be uncertain. Follow through on the part that belongs to you.
